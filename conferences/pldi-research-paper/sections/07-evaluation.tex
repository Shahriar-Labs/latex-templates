\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We ask four questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1:] Does guarded specialization improve steady-state performance?
  \item[RQ2:] Which part of the design produces the improvement?
  \item[RQ3:] What are its code-size and compilation costs?
  \item[RQ4:] How does it behave when handler shapes change?
\end{description}

\subsection{Methodology}

The benchmark suite contains 18 programs in five groups: parsers, asynchronous
services, probabilistic models, search workloads, and effect microbenchmarks.
Programs range from 0.8 to 41 K source lines and execute between $1.7\times
10^6$ and $2.1\times10^9$ operations. We compare \emph{Generic}, which uses
segmented-stack handlers, with \emph{Cache}, which adds a polymorphic inline
cache but retains generic continuation capture, and \system.

Measurements use a 16-core 3.4 GHz x86-64 machine with 64 GiB of memory,
frequency scaling disabled. Each configuration receives 10 warm-up executions
followed by 30 measured executions in a fresh process. We report geometric
means and 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals, following established guidance
for managed-language experiments \cite{georges2007statistically}. Builds and
inputs are pinned by a declarative environment \cite{dolstra2010nixos}.

\subsection{Steady-State Performance}

\Cref{tab:results} answers RQ1. \system is faster than Generic on all 18
programs, with speedups from 1.08$\times$ to 2.41$\times$ and a geometric mean
of 1.46$\times$. It outperforms Cache on 16 programs. The two ties are async
servers whose time is dominated by kernel I/O; specialization still reduces
CPU time by 9\% and 11\%. Benefits track eliminated handler searches only
loosely ($r=0.61$), because avoiding continuation allocation is more valuable
than avoiding a short search.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

We disable one optimization at a time. Specializing dispatch while leaving
capture generic retains only 37\% of \system's benefit. Moving guards inside
loops loses 19\%; disabling scalar replacement loses 12\%. Descriptor-vector
interning changes execution time by less than 1\% on shallow programs but saves
7\% on probabilistic models with three nested handlers. Thus the main result
does not come from a richer inline cache: direct-style continuations are
essential.

\subsection{Cost and Dynamic Behavior}

Specialization increases text size by 3.7\% geometrically (range 0.8--8.9\%)
and compilation time by 6.2\% (RQ3). Reconstruction metadata adds 0.4\% to
binary size. Peak compiler memory rises by 4.5\%. The region benefit model
rejects 38\% of candidate sites, mostly cold sites and regions with large
control-flow duplication.

For RQ4, we switch each benchmark among one, two, or four handler shapes every
$10^2$ to $10^6$ operations. A failed guard costs 31 ns (median) including
reconstruction. With phases of at least $10^4$ operations, \system adapts after
one recompilation and retains 91\% of its stable-workload speedup. At the most
adversarial $10^2$ interval, the recompilation cap activates and performance is
within 2.6\% of Generic. No workload creates more than two specialized versions
per site.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

The prototype targets one strict language and x86-64; different continuation
representations or weaker memory models may change costs. Our suite emphasizes
effect-heavy code and should not predict whole-application speedups when
effects are cold. Profile-directed choices can depend on inputs, which we
mitigate with held-out evaluation inputs and the dynamic fallback. Finally,
the semantic proof covers the core transformation, not LLVM or the garbage
collector. We reduce this gap with map verification and differential tests that
force every guard to fail, but end-to-end compiler verification remains future
work.
