  \noindent{\bfseries Abstract.}\hspace{0.4em}
  Throughput has long served as the primary yardstick for residential access quality, yet
  interactive and real-time applications are increasingly sensitive to delay rather than
  raw capacity. We present a six-week active-measurement campaign covering
  $n=3{,}116$ vantage points across 41 residential ISPs in 12 countries, quantifying how
  much round-trip latency inflates when an access link is saturated by ordinary bulk
  traffic. We introduce the Latency Inflation Ratio (LIR), a normalized metric relating
  loaded and idle round-trip time, and measure it separately for fiber, cable, DSL, fixed
  wireless, satellite, and mobile access technologies. We find that fiber links exhibit a
  median LIR of $0.06$, roughly $3.5\times$ lower than cable and DSL, while fixed wireless
  and low-earth-orbit satellite links show the heaviest tails, with 95th-percentile LIR
  exceeding $1.1$. These results suggest that access-layer buffering and scheduling
  policy, more than raw bandwidth, dominate the loaded-latency experience for a
  substantial share of residential users, and we discuss implications for active queue
  management and low-latency transport deployment.
  \vspace{4pt}

  \noindent{\small\itshape Keywords: bufferbloat, latency inflation, active measurement,
  residential broadband, access networks}
