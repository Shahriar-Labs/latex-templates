  \noindent{\bfseries Abstract.}\hspace{0.4em}
  Throughput has long served as the primary yardstick for residential access quality, yet
  interactive and real-time applications are increasingly sensitive to delay rather than
  raw capacity. We present a six-week active-measurement campaign covering
  $n=3{,}116$ vantage points across 41 residential ISPs in 12 countries, quantifying how
  much round-trip latency inflates when an access link is saturated by ordinary bulk
  traffic. We introduce the Latency Inflation Ratio (LIR), a normalized metric relating
  loaded and idle round-trip time, and measure it separately for fiber, cable, DSL, fixed
  wireless, satellite, and mobile access technologies. We find that fiber links exhibit a
  median LIR of $0.06$, roughly $3.5\times$ lower than cable and DSL, while fixed wireless
  and low-earth-orbit satellite links show the heaviest tails, with 95th-percentile LIR
  exceeding $1.1$. These results suggest that access-layer buffering and scheduling
  policy, more than raw bandwidth, dominate the loaded-latency experience for a
  substantial share of residential users, and we discuss implications for active queue
  management and low-latency transport deployment.

  residential broadband, access networks}

\section{Introduction}
Household internet access has, for two decades, been marketed and benchmarked almost
exclusively on downstream throughput. Regulatory broadband maps, ISP advertising, and
most consumer speed-test tools report megabits per second and little else. Yet a growing
share of residential traffic is delay-sensitive: videoconferencing, cloud gaming, voice
telephony, and remote collaborative editing all degrade sharply when round-trip time
grows, independent of whether the link's nominal capacity is saturated. The well-known
\emph{bufferbloat} phenomenon, in which deep and poorly managed queues in access-network
equipment allow latency to climb by hundreds of milliseconds under load, has been
documented in individual devices and small testbeds, but its prevalence across access
technologies, ISPs, and geographies at panel scale remains comparatively under-measured.

This paper reports on a large, multi-vantage active-measurement study designed to close
that gap. Software probes deployed on volunteer home gateways and companion devices
issue paired idle and loaded round-trip-time measurements, allowing us to isolate the
latency contribution of access-link saturation from baseline propagation and processing
delay. We define a single normalized metric, the Latency Inflation Ratio (LIR), and
compute it consistently across six access technologies spanning fiber, cable, digital
subscriber line (DSL), fixed wireless access (FWA), low-earth-orbit (LEO) satellite, and
macro-cellular mobile networks.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we describe a measurement methodology and
panel design that yields directly comparable loaded-latency estimates across
heterogeneous access technologies and commercial ISPs. Second, we report LIR
distributions from a six-week campaign covering 3{,}116 vantage points in 41 ISPs
across 12 countries, the largest cross-technology comparison of loaded latency we are
aware of. Third, we analyze which technologies and deployment patterns are most
associated with severe latency inflation, and discuss what our findings imply for the
ongoing rollout of active queue management (AQM) and low-latency transport mechanisms
such as L4S.

our work relative to prior bufferbloat and access-network measurement studies.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes our measurement architecture and the LIR metric.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} details the deployment and dataset. Section~\ref{sec:results}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Early work on queueing delay in home networks established that consumer routers and
cable/DSL modems frequently ship with oversized, statically configured buffers, producing
latency spikes far larger than would be predicted by link capacity and offered load
alone~\cite{whitfield2017bufferbloat}. Subsequent audits of AQM deployment in shipped home
gateways found that, even where AQM algorithms were available, default configurations
frequently left them disabled~\cite{bellini2016aqm}. Our own prior work examined last-mile
latency in a ten-country panel but was limited to idle-network measurements and did not
isolate the loaded-latency contribution that is the focus of the present
study~\cite{marchetti2019lastmile}.

A parallel line of work has focused on measurement methodology itself.
Bauer and Solvang survey active-probing techniques for residential broadband and catalog
the confounds that arise from cross-traffic, NAT middleboxes, and CPE-level
scheduling~\cite{bauer2015probing}; we adopt several of their recommended controls,
including paired idle/loaded sampling and per-probe outlier filtering. Rahman and Wei
decompose end-to-end web latency across the access path and show that access-link
queueing, rather than core-network or server-side delay, dominates the variance in
last-mile response time for a majority of residential connections~\cite{rahman2018cdn}.

Technology-specific measurement has grown alongside the diversification of residential
access. Sundaram and Natarajan characterize fixed wireless access performance across
spectrum bands and find substantial variance tied to sector loading~\cite{sundaram2020fwa}.
Ferreira and Petrova provide one of the first broad looks at LEO satellite latency and
jitter, reporting idle latencies far lower than legacy geostationary satellite but
significant variability under contention~\cite{ferreira2021satellite}. Okonkwo and
Whitfield track DSL performance longitudinally as fiber overbuild reduces the subscriber
base sharing legacy copper plant~\cite{okonkwo2021dsl}, and Natarajan and Okonkwo measure
cellular latency under load across 4G and early 5G macro deployments~\cite{natarajan2019mobile}.
Petrova and Bellini connect access-link delay directly to interactive quality-of-experience
outcomes~\cite{petrova2018qoe}, and Wei and Zhao report early field signals from L4S
deployment trials~\cite{wei2022l4s}. Our study differs from this body of work by measuring
a consistent metric across all six access technologies within a single, contemporaneous
panel, and at a scale that supports cross-technology and cross-ISP comparison.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Measurement Architecture}
Our measurement panel, which we refer to as the Meridian Measurement Panel, consists of
lightweight software probes running on volunteer home routers and companion single-board
devices. Each probe is opt-in, reports only aggregated latency statistics rather than raw
packet contents, and is paired at enrollment with self-reported and ISP-confirmed access
technology and nominal plan speed. Probes communicate with a small set of geographically
distributed collector nodes over UDP and TLS-protected control channels; collectors never
initiate connections into the home network.

\subsection{Loaded/Idle Probing Protocol}
Each measurement cycle consists of three phases. In the idle phase, a probe issues 30
lightweight UDP echo probes at 200\,ms intervals while no other flows are active on the
link, and records the median round-trip time as $\mathrm{RTT}^{\text{idle}}$. In the load
phase, the probe opens a synthetic bulk TCP transfer toward a nearby collector, ramps it
to saturate the measured link in either the downstream or upstream direction, and
continues issuing the same UDP echo probes in parallel, recording the median as
$\mathrm{RTT}^{\text{loaded}}$. In the cooldown phase, the bulk transfer is torn down and
the link is allowed to drain before the next cycle begins. Downstream- and
upstream-saturating cycles are run in alternation to characterize both directions.

\subsection{Latency Inflation Ratio}
We define the Latency Inflation Ratio for probe $i$ and cycle $j$ as the fractional
increase in round-trip time observed when the access link is saturated, relative to its
idle baseline:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{LIR}_{i,j} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{RTT}_{i,j}^{\text{loaded}} - \mathrm{RTT}_{i,j}^{\text{idle}}}{\mathrm{RTT}_{i,j}^{\text{idle}}}
  \label{eq:lir}
\end{equation}
A probe experiencing no additional queueing delay under load has $\mathrm{LIR}\approx 0$,
while a probe whose loaded RTT doubles has $\mathrm{LIR}=1$. We compute
Equation~\eqref{eq:lir} per cycle and aggregate to a per-probe median and 95th-percentile
over the full campaign, requiring at least $n \geq 30$ valid cycle pairs per probe for
inclusion. Cycles with packet loss exceeding $5\%$ during the idle phase, indicative of an
already-congested or otherwise unhealthy link, are discarded to avoid conflating baseline
instability with load-induced inflation.

\subsection{Ethics and Privacy}
Panel enrollment is opt-in and reviewed under Nexa University's human-subjects protocol.
Probes record only timing metadata and coarse ISP/technology labels; no packet payloads,
destination hostnames outside the measurement infrastructure, or household-identifying
information are collected. Reported results are aggregated at the technology and ISP
level throughout this paper.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We ran the measurement campaign for six consecutive weeks, from probes already enrolled
in the Meridian Measurement Panel as of the start of the campaign, supplemented by a
short recruitment push to improve coverage of fixed wireless and satellite access, which
were underrepresented in the existing panel. The final dataset comprises 3{,}116 vantage
points across 41 residential ISPs in 12 countries, spanning six access technologies:
fiber (GPON and XGS-PON), cable (DOCSIS 3.1), DSL (VDSL2), fixed wireless access (5G
FWA), low-earth-orbit satellite, and macro-cellular mobile (4G/5G). Each probe completed
both downstream- and upstream-saturating cycles roughly every 40 minutes throughout the
campaign, yielding just over 48 million completed load/idle cycle pairs after quality
filtering. Compute and bandwidth for collector infrastructure were donated by Apex Cloud;
panel recruitment and device logistics were supported by a grant from the Meridian
Research Foundation, and Nimbus Networks contributed a subset of fixed-wireless vantage
points from its own volunteer subscriber base.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes per-technology LIR and loss statistics aggregated over
the full campaign. Fiber access shows both the lowest median inflation ($0.06$) and the
tightest tail ($p95 = 0.19$), consistent with symmetric, lightly buffered PON access
architectures and comparatively little contention among subscribers sharing a given
optical distribution point. Cable and DSL cluster together at roughly $3$--$4\times$
fiber's median inflation, which we attribute to a combination of larger static buffers in
deployed cable modems and, for DSL, older CPE that in our panel rarely had modern AQM
enabled. Fixed wireless, satellite, and mobile technologies show the heaviest tails: LEO
satellite's median LIR ($0.29$) is comparable to cable, but its 95th percentile ($1.35$)
and loss rate ($1.86\%$) are the highest of any technology we measured, reflecting
episodic contention and handoff effects between satellite beams.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our results reinforce a claim that has circulated informally in the operator and AQM
communities but has lacked large-scale cross-technology evidence: nominal link capacity
is a poor predictor of loaded-latency behavior, and access-technology and CPE buffering
policy dominate instead. Two operational implications follow. First, ISPs and CPE vendors
evaluating AQM deployment should prioritize cable and DSL fleets, where our data show the
largest gap between currently observed and fiber-like achievable inflation. Second, the
heavy tails we observe for fixed wireless and satellite access suggest that
worst-case, rather than median, latency behavior should inform application-level
adaptation for those technologies, since a non-trivial share of sessions on those links
will encounter substantial queueing delay regardless of typical performance.

Several limitations bound how far these findings generalize. Our panel is
volunteer-recruited and, despite a targeted recruitment push for underrepresented
technologies, likely skews toward more technically engaged subscribers, who may be more
likely to have upgraded CPE or enabled available AQM features; if so, our estimates are
conservative relative to the full subscriber population. Geographic coverage, while
spanning 12 countries, is concentrated in urban and suburban areas, and rural access
characteristics, particularly for DSL and satellite, may differ materially. Finally, our
loaded-latency protocol uses a single synthetic bulk flow per cycle; real household
traffic often involves multiple concurrent flows with different scheduling priorities,
which could produce either more or less severe inflation than we observe, depending on
CPE queue management. We view longitudinal tracking of the same panel through AQM and
L4S rollout, together with multi-flow loading protocols, as natural next steps.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a six-week, multi-vantage active-measurement study of loaded-latency
behavior across six residential access technologies, introducing the Latency Inflation
Ratio as a simple, comparable metric for quantifying access-link queueing delay under
realistic load. Across 3{,}116 vantage points in 41 ISPs and 12 countries, we find that
fiber access delivers substantially lower and more consistent loaded latency than cable,
DSL, fixed wireless, satellite, or mobile access, and that fixed wireless and satellite
technologies in particular exhibit heavy-tailed inflation that is not well summarized by
median statistics alone. We hope this dataset and methodology support both future
measurement work and more latency-aware benchmarking of residential access quality.
