\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our results reinforce a claim that has circulated informally in the operator and AQM
communities but has lacked large-scale cross-technology evidence: nominal link capacity
is a poor predictor of loaded-latency behavior, and access-technology and CPE buffering
policy dominate instead. Two operational implications follow. First, ISPs and CPE vendors
evaluating AQM deployment should prioritize cable and DSL fleets, where our data show the
largest gap between currently observed and fiber-like achievable inflation. Second, the
heavy tails we observe for fixed wireless and satellite access suggest that
worst-case, rather than median, latency behavior should inform application-level
adaptation for those technologies, since a non-trivial share of sessions on those links
will encounter substantial queueing delay regardless of typical performance.

Several limitations bound how far these findings generalize. Our panel is
volunteer-recruited and, despite a targeted recruitment push for underrepresented
technologies, likely skews toward more technically engaged subscribers, who may be more
likely to have upgraded CPE or enabled available AQM features; if so, our estimates are
conservative relative to the full subscriber population. Geographic coverage, while
spanning 12 countries, is concentrated in urban and suburban areas, and rural access
characteristics, particularly for DSL and satellite, may differ materially. Finally, our
loaded-latency protocol uses a single synthetic bulk flow per cycle; real household
traffic often involves multiple concurrent flows with different scheduling priorities,
which could produce either more or less severe inflation than we observe, depending on
CPE queue management. We view longitudinal tracking of the same panel through AQM and
L4S rollout, together with multi-flow loading protocols, as natural next steps.
