\section{Introduction}

Legged machines can traverse gaps, stairs, and debris that defeat wheeled platforms, but
their mobility depends on a fragile perception--planning interface. State-of-the-art
locomotion systems combine learned policies \cite{hwangbo2019skills,miki2022wild} or
model-predictive control (MPC) \cite{grandia2023perceptive} with a robot-centric terrain
map. Most controllers consume the map as if its cells were equally current and reliable.
In practice, a swinging leg occludes the camera, dust erases depth returns, and rapid body
motion moves important footholds outside the field of view. The map remains numerically
dense while the evidence supporting it quietly expires.

This paper studies the following question: \emph{how should a legged robot move when its
best geometric map may be wrong?} Our answer is \method{}, a short-horizon terrain memory
whose output is a pair $(\hat{H},R)$: an estimated elevation map and a calibrated risk map.
The representation makes missing evidence explicit, incorporates contact events as local
geometric measurements, and permits the controller to choose whether uncertain progress is
worth the predicted failure probability.

The system, summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, contributes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a recurrent, robot-centric fusion model that preserves terrain evidence across
        asynchronous observations without freezing stale geometry;
  \item a cell-wise risk target derived from reconstruction error, visibility age, and
        contact inconsistency, calibrated for use as a control cost; and
  \item a risk-constrained foothold objective with a simple fallback behavior that remains
        stable when the entire forward view is unavailable.
\end{itemize}
The numerical results in this example manuscript are clearly illustrative, but the
experimental protocol, equations, reporting structure, and implementation details are
specified to make the document a complete, adaptable research-paper source.
