\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Machine-learning evaluation usually treats a held-out source sample as a proxy for
future performance.  That proxy fails when deployment changes the distribution of
inputs.  Examples include a medical model transferred between hospitals, a vision
model exposed to a new sensor, or a ranking system serving a new region.  Dataset
shift is therefore not merely a predictive problem; it is a problem of deciding
whether the available evidence supports deployment \citep{quionero2009dataset}.

Under \emph{covariate shift}, the conditional law of the outcome given the input is
stable while the input marginal changes.  Importance weighting then identifies the
target risk from labeled source data.  Classical work develops density-ratio
estimators and weighted learning procedures \citep{sugiyama2007direct}; weighted
conformal methods similarly recover predictive coverage under shift
\citep{tibshirani2019conformal}.  In contrast, we focus on a narrower operational
question:
\begin{quote}
\small
Given several already-trained predictors, can a held-out source sample certify
that the chosen predictor's target risk is below a stated tolerance?
\end{quote}

The distinction matters.  An unbiased or consistent estimator need not be a valid
upper bound, and selecting the smallest estimate among many candidates invalidates
a pointwise guarantee.  Our construction addresses both issues with a simultaneous
concentration bound.  It makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a distribution-free target-risk certificate for bounded losses and an
        independently estimated, clipped density ratio;
  \item a uniform version that remains valid after selecting among a finite model
        family, plus a variance-adaptive refinement; and
  \item an auditable deployment rule and deterministic stress tests that expose
        the effects of sample size, overlap, and multiplicity.
\end{itemize}

The message is deliberately modest.  A certificate cannot repair a violation of
covariate shift or a lack of overlap.  It instead converts explicit assumptions
into a finite-sample statement and becomes conservative when those assumptions are
weak.
