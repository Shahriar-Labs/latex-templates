    \begin{abstract}
      Predictive systems are commonly trained and validated on one population but
      deployed on another.  Importance weighting corrects the resulting covariate
      shift asymptotically, yet a point estimate of target risk is not a deployment
      certificate.  We study finite-sample upper certificates for bounded loss when
      the target-to-source density ratio is bounded and may be estimated from
      unlabeled data.  A single held-out sample yields a simultaneous certificate
      for a finite family of predictors, allowing a practitioner to choose the most
      useful model whose target risk is certified below a prescribed tolerance.
      The guarantee separates three costs: observed weighted loss, statistical
      uncertainty, and density-ratio error.  We also give an empirical-Bernstein
      refinement, a selection rule, and a deterministic stress test that makes the
      dependence on sample size, shift severity, and model multiplicity explicit.
      The result is intentionally simple: every assumption is auditable, the proof
      fits on one page, and failure modes are visible in the certificate itself.
    \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Machine-learning evaluation usually treats a held-out source sample as a proxy for
future performance.  That proxy fails when deployment changes the distribution of
inputs.  Examples include a medical model transferred between hospitals, a vision
model exposed to a new sensor, or a ranking system serving a new region.  Dataset
shift is therefore not merely a predictive problem; it is a problem of deciding
whether the available evidence supports deployment \citep{quionero2009dataset}.

Under \emph{covariate shift}, the conditional law of the outcome given the input is
stable while the input marginal changes.  Importance weighting then identifies the
target risk from labeled source data.  Classical work develops density-ratio
estimators and weighted learning procedures \citep{sugiyama2007direct}; weighted
conformal methods similarly recover predictive coverage under shift
\citep{tibshirani2019conformal}.  In contrast, we focus on a narrower operational
question:
\begin{quote}
\small
Given several already-trained predictors, can a held-out source sample certify
that the chosen predictor's target risk is below a stated tolerance?
\end{quote}

The distinction matters.  An unbiased or consistent estimator need not be a valid
upper bound, and selecting the smallest estimate among many candidates invalidates
a pointwise guarantee.  Our construction addresses both issues with a simultaneous
concentration bound.  It makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a distribution-free target-risk certificate for bounded losses and an
        independently estimated, clipped density ratio;
  \item a uniform version that remains valid after selecting among a finite model
        family, plus a variance-adaptive refinement; and
  \item an auditable deployment rule and deterministic stress tests that expose
        the effects of sample size, overlap, and multiplicity.
\end{itemize}

The message is deliberately modest.  A certificate cannot repair a violation of
covariate shift or a lack of overlap.  It instead converts explicit assumptions
into a finite-sample statement and becomes conservative when those assumptions are
weak.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Dataset shift and importance weighting.}
Covariate shift is one member of a broader taxonomy of train--test mismatch
\citep{quionero2009dataset}.  Direct density-ratio estimation avoids separately
estimating two high-dimensional densities \citep{sugiyama2007direct}.  Kernel
two-sample methods provide useful diagnostics, although failure to reject equality
does not establish the covariate-shift assumption \citep{gretton2012kernel}.
Distributionally robust optimization targets performance across a prespecified set
of distributions or groups \citep{sagawa2020distributionally}; our objective is
instead a confidence bound for one target population.

\paragraph{Distribution-free uncertainty.}
Conformal prediction gives marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability
\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution}.  Weighted conformal prediction
extends this logic to covariate shift when likelihood ratios are known
\citep{tibshirani2019conformal}.  Risk-controlling prediction generalizes coverage
to bounded losses \citep{angelopoulos2024risk}.  Our bound is a compact
importance-weighted analogue aimed at post-training model selection rather than
construction of prediction sets.

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
Abstaining systems trade coverage for conditional accuracy
\citep{geifman2017selective}.  Each abstention threshold can be treated as a
candidate in our finite family, so selection over thresholds is covered without
reusing the certification sample for training.  The guarantee concerns the
unconditional bounded loss specified by the user; conditional risk requires a
separate treatment of the random acceptance probability.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $P$ be a source distribution and $Q$ a target distribution on
$\cX\times\cY$.  For a predictor $f\in\cF$, let
$\ell(f(X),Y)\in[0,1]$ be a measurable loss and define
\begin{equation}
  \Risk_Q(f)=\E_{(X,Y)\sim Q}\!\left[\ell(f(X),Y)\right].
  \label{eq:target-risk}
\end{equation}
The predictors $\cF_K=\{f_1,\ldots,f_K\}$ are trained without the certification
sample $S=\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}P$.

\begin{assumption}[Covariate shift]
\label{ass:covariate}
$P(Y\mid X)=Q(Y\mid X)$ almost surely, and $Q_X$ is absolutely continuous with
respect to $P_X$.
\end{assumption}

The corresponding density ratio is $w(x)=dQ_X/dP_X(x)$.  By a change of measure,
Assumption~\ref{ass:covariate} implies
\begin{equation}
  \Risk_Q(f)=\E_P\!\left[w(X)\ell(f(X),Y)\right].
  \label{eq:change-measure}
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Auditable overlap and ratio error]
\label{ass:ratio}
An estimator $\wh w:\cX\to[0,B]$, fitted independently of $S$, satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \E_{X\sim P_X}\abs{\wh w(X)-w(X)}\leq \varepsilon_w
  \label{eq:ratio-error}
\end{equation}
for declared constants $B<\infty$ and $\varepsilon_w\geq0$.
\end{assumption}

Independence can be obtained with a three-way split: predictor training, ratio
estimation using unlabeled source and target inputs, and certification.  The error
budget $\varepsilon_w$ may come from domain knowledge, a separate validation study,
or a high-probability bound for a particular ratio estimator.  Treating an
unvalidated estimate as though $\varepsilon_w=0$ is not justified.

\section{Simultaneous Certificates}
\label{sec:method}

For each candidate define the estimated weighted risk
\begin{equation}
  \wh R_k=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \wh w(X_i)\ell(f_k(X_i),Y_i).
  \label{eq:empirical-risk}
\end{equation}
The following result is the core certificate.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-family target-risk certificate]
\label{thm:certificate}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:covariate}--\ref{ass:ratio}, for any
$\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over $S$, simultaneously
for every $k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$,
\begin{equation}
  \Risk_Q(f_k)\leq U_k
  :=\min\!\left\{1,\ \wh R_k+\varepsilon_w
       +B\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{2n}}\right\}.
  \label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the inequality remains valid for any data-dependent index
$\wh k=\wh k(S)$ taking values in $\{1,\ldots,K\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $k$ and condition on the fitted predictors and ratio estimator.  Put
$Z_{ik}=\wh w(X_i)\ell(f_k(X_i),Y_i)\in[0,B]$.  Hoeffding's inequality gives
\[
  \Pp\!\left(\E_P[Z_{ik}]-\wh R_k>B
  \sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{2n}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{K}.
\]
Moreover, boundedness of the loss and \cref{eq:ratio-error} imply
\[
  \E_P[w\ell_k]
  \leq \E_P[\wh w\ell_k]+\E_P\abs{w-\wh w}
  \leq \E_P[Z_{ik}]+\varepsilon_w.
\]
Apply a union bound over $k$ and use \cref{eq:change-measure}.  Clipping the upper
bound at one is valid because the loss lies in $[0,1]$.  Since all $K$ inequalities
hold on one event, substituting the selected index $\wh k(S)$ preserves the claim.
\end{proof}

The radius scales as $B\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)/n}$.  Thus poor source--target overlap
is costly through $B$, while searching a larger finite family has only logarithmic
cost.  The additive $\varepsilon_w$ term does not vanish with more certification
labels; better labels cannot compensate for an unvalidated ratio estimator.

\subsection{Variance-adaptive refinement}

Hoeffding's bound ignores that the observed weighted losses may have low variance.
Let
\[
  V_k=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{ik}-\wh R_k)^2,
  \qquad n\geq2.
\]
An empirical-Bernstein inequality \citep{maurer2009empirical} yields the alternative
certificate
\begin{align}
  \rho_k^{\mathrm{EB}}
  &=\sqrt{\frac{2V_k\log(2K/\delta)}{n}}
    +\frac{7B\log(2K/\delta)}{3(n-1)}, \nonumber\\
  U_k^{\mathrm{EB}}
  &=\min\!\left\{1,\ \wh R_k+\varepsilon_w+\rho_k^{\mathrm{EB}}\right\}.
  \label{eq:eb-certificate}
\end{align}
The same union-bound argument makes \cref{eq:eb-certificate} simultaneous.  A
predeclared choice of the smaller of \cref{eq:certificate} and
\cref{eq:eb-certificate} requires allocating failure probability to both bounds;
one may replace $\delta$ by $\delta/2$ in each and take their minimum.

\subsection{Certified model selection}

Suppose $a_k$ is a utility score computed without $S$ (for example, target-input
coverage for an abstaining predictor), and $\tau$ is the maximum acceptable target
risk.  Define
\begin{equation}
  \wh k\in\argmax_{k:U_k\leq\tau}a_k,
  \label{eq:selection}
\end{equation}
returning ``no certified model'' if the feasible set is empty.  By
\cref{thm:certificate}, any returned model satisfies
$\Risk_Q(f_{\wh k})\leq\tau$ with probability at least $1-\delta$.

\section{Deterministic Stress Tests}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the algebra of the certificate rather than claiming an
empirical comparison.  Every table entry is obtained by direct substitution into
\cref{eq:certificate}; no random trial or hidden dataset is involved.  This is
useful because the dominant tradeoffs can be inspected before collecting labels.

\subsection{What a deployment decision looks like}

Consider a risk tolerance $\tau=0.15$ and the first column of
\cref{tab:sample-size}.  With $n=500$, a candidate having weighted loss $.073$
cannot be certified even if the ratio is known exactly.  At $n=2000$, the bound is
approximately $.150$ before ratio error and becomes infeasible for any positive
$\varepsilon_w$.  At $n=8000$, an error budget as large as $.038$ remains feasible.
The calculation gives a concrete data-acquisition target: gather more certification
labels, improve overlap, validate the ratio more tightly, or decline deployment.

\section{Practical Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The theorem's probability statement is only as meaningful as its assumptions.
Before issuing a certificate, we recommend the following audit.

\paragraph{Check support, not only average fit.}
Inspect the distribution of $\wh w(X)$ on held-out source inputs and the fraction
clipped at $B$.  Frequent clipping suggests that the numerical guarantee is tied to
a poor approximation of $w$; this should increase $\varepsilon_w$ or stop the
analysis.  Compare source and target representations using domain-relevant plots
and two-sample diagnostics \citep{gretton2012kernel}.

\paragraph{Probe conditional stability.}
Covariate shift asserts equality of $P(Y\mid X)$ and $Q(Y\mid X)$, which cannot in
general be verified from unlabeled target inputs.  Small, carefully sampled target
label audits are more informative than elaborate marginal tests.  If conditional
shift is plausible, add a separately justified sensitivity budget
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cond}}$ to the right side of \cref{eq:certificate}.

\paragraph{Preserve the holdout.}
The model family, loss, clipping level, and bound variant must be fixed before
examining certification outcomes.  If the sample is reused iteratively, the simple
union bound no longer describes the search.  Fresh data, nested splitting, or tools
for adaptive data analysis are then needed.

\paragraph{Choose the loss to match the harm.}
For classification error, $\ell=\mathbf{1}\{f(X)\neq Y\}$ is immediate.  Cost-
sensitive or subgroup-weighted harms can also be normalized to $[0,1]$.  A low
average loss does not imply low risk for every subgroup; certify each prespecified
group separately and include those certificates in the multiplicity budget.

\section{Limitations and Broader Impact}
\label{sec:limitations}

Three limitations are fundamental.  First, the result assumes covariate shift; it
does not detect changes in the outcome mechanism.  Second, the declared ratio-error
budget is external to the theorem.  A practitioner who sets it optimistically can
produce a mathematically correct calculation with an invalid premise.  Third,
bounded importance weights trade bias against variance: clipping stabilizes the
estimate but can enlarge $\varepsilon_w$.

The framework can support safer deployment by making uncertainty and abstention
explicit.  It can also create false reassurance if a single population-average
certificate is used to dismiss subgroup harms or if lack of overlap is hidden by
aggressive clipping.  Any deployment report should therefore publish the target
population definition, loss, split protocol, weight diagnostics, $B$,
$\varepsilon_w$, candidate count, and failure probability.  High-impact decisions
still require domain review and ongoing monitoring.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Importance weighting identifies target risk under covariate shift, but deployment
requires uncertainty around that estimate.  A held-out sample and a bounded
density-ratio estimate yield a simultaneous finite-sample certificate whose three
terms have clear interpretations: measured weighted loss, sampling uncertainty,
and ratio error.  Because the event is uniform over a declared finite family, the
certificate survives model selection.  Its conservatism is useful information:
failure to certify identifies whether the next effort should target more labels,
better overlap, better ratio validation, or a decision not to deploy.

\section{Additional Derivations}
\label{app:derivations}

\subsection{A bound with a random ratio-error guarantee}

Assumption~\ref{ass:ratio} states a deterministic error budget after conditioning
on the fitted ratio estimator.  Often the ratio procedure instead supplies
\begin{equation}
  \Pp\!\left(
    \E_{P_X}\abs{\wh w(X)-w(X)}\leq\varepsilon_w
  \right)\geq1-\delta_w.
  \label{eq:random-ratio}
\end{equation}
If ratio fitting data and certification data are independent, combine
\cref{eq:random-ratio} with \cref{thm:certificate} using a union bound.  With
probability at least $1-\delta_w-\delta_c$, simultaneously for all $k$,
\begin{equation}
  \Risk_Q(f_k)\leq \wh R_k+\varepsilon_w
  +B\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta_c)}{2n}}.
\end{equation}
This form prevents the confidence used to validate the ratio from disappearing
inside a nominally deterministic constant.

\subsection{Bias introduced by clipping}

Let $\wt w(x)=\min\{w(x),B\}$ and abbreviate
$\ell_f=\ell(f(X),Y)$.  Even with the true ratio available, clipping changes the
estimand.  Since $0\leq\ell_f\leq1$,
\begin{align}
  \Risk_Q(f)-\E_P[\wt w(X)\ell_f]
  &=\E_P[(w(X)-B)_+\ell_f]\\
  &\leq\E_P[(w(X)-B)_+].
\end{align}
Thus the clipping tail $\E_P[(w-B)_+]$ is a valid contribution to
$\varepsilon_w$.  The expression also shows why reporting only the cap $B$ is
insufficient: the amount of target mass represented by clipped observations
matters.

\subsection{Prespecified groups}

For groups $G_1,\ldots,G_m\subseteq\cX$ with $Q_X(G_j)>0$, conditional group risk
can be written as a ratio,
\begin{equation}
  \Risk_{Q,j}(f)=
  \frac{\E_P[w(X)\mathbf{1}\{X\in G_j\}\ell(f(X),Y)]}
       {\E_P[w(X)\mathbf{1}\{X\in G_j\}]}.
\end{equation}
Certifying this quantity requires simultaneous control of both numerator and
denominator; plugging a noisy denominator into \cref{eq:certificate} is not valid.
A conservative construction uses an upper confidence bound for the numerator and a
strictly positive lower confidence bound for the denominator, with failure
probability allocated across all $mK$ pairs.  Small target group mass makes the
denominator bound weak, correctly reflecting limited evidence.

\section{Reproducibility Checklist}
\label{app:checklist}

The following information is sufficient to reproduce every numerical value in the
main text and should accompany an empirical application.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bound:} \cref{eq:certificate}, with natural logarithms.
  \item \textbf{Stress-test constants:} $K=20$, $\delta=.05$, $B=2$,
        $\wh R_k=.073$, and $n\in\{500,2000,8000\}$.
  \item \textbf{Rounding:} compute in full precision and round to three decimals
        only for display.
  \item \textbf{Figure constants:} the same $K,\delta,B$, with
        $n\in\{250,500,1000,2000,4000,8000\}$.
  \item \textbf{Randomness and compute:} none; the tables and figure are
        deterministic evaluations of a closed-form expression.
  \item \textbf{Empirical extension:} disclose data provenance, all three split
        sizes, candidate construction, ratio-estimation method, clipping diagnostics,
        the derivation of $\varepsilon_w$, and every selection made after viewing
        certification outcomes.
\end{itemize}
