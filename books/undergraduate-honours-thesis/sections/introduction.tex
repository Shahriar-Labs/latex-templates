\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Modern web and media applications route the majority of user requests through edge caches
rather than origin servers, since retrieving a cached object at a nearby point of presence is
an order of magnitude faster than a round trip to a centralised data centre. The effectiveness
of this architecture rests almost entirely on one decision, repeated billions of times a day:
when the cache is full and a new object arrives, which existing object should be evicted?

Classical eviction policies such as Least Recently Used (LRU) and Least Frequently Used (LFU)
were designed decades ago for memory and disk caching, where request patterns were comparatively
uniform. Edge content delivery traffic looks nothing like that: it is heavily skewed (a small
number of objects account for most requests), bursty (popularity shifts within minutes during
a news event or product launch), and heterogeneous in object size. Section~\ref{sec:lit-gap}
argues that this mismatch leaves measurable performance on the table.

\begin{researchquestion}
\textbf{Research Question.} Can a lightweight, per-object adaptive scoring policy improve edge
cache hit ratio and latency over LRU and LFU baselines under realistic skewed workloads, at
computational overhead low enough for line-rate deployment?
\end{researchquestion}

\section{Objectives}
This thesis pursues three concrete objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Design an eviction policy, the Gradient-Weighted Cache (GWC), that combines recency,
        frequency, and object-size signals into a single incrementally updated utility score.
  \item Build a reproducible simulation testbed (EdgeSim) capable of replaying synthetic
        Zipfian-distributed request traces across a multi-node edge topology.
  \item Empirically compare GWC against LRU and LFU on hit ratio, byte hit ratio, mean
        latency, and per-request computational overhead.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A formal description of the GWC scoring function and its $O(1)$ amortised update rule
        (Section~\ref{sec:gwc-design}).
  \item An open, parameterised workload generator for edge-caching research, calibrated
        against publicly reported CDN traffic skew ($\theta \approx 0.8$--$1.0$).
  \item An empirical evaluation across twelve simulated edge nodes showing a 17.5 percentage
        point hit-ratio improvement over LRU (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{keytakeaway}
GWC is not a learned cache: it has no training phase, no model to serve, and updates in
constant time per access. The thesis's central claim is that this is enough to close most
of the gap to more expensive adaptive schemes.
\end{keytakeaway}

\section{Thesis Structure}
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature-review} reviews prior work on cache replacement policies and
adaptive/learned caching. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the GWC algorithm, the
EdgeSim testbed, and the experimental protocol. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the
quantitative results. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets those results and discusses
limitations. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes and outlines future work.
