\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
This thesis set out to test whether a lightweight, per-object adaptive eviction policy could
improve edge cache performance over static baselines without the operational overhead of a
learned cache. The Gradient-Weighted Cache (GWC), introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:gwc-design}, improved hit ratio by 17.5 percentage points over LRU and
12.1 points over LFU on a stationary Zipfian workload, and retained a substantial advantage
under simulated popularity drift, at a per-request overhead under 0.11\,microseconds. These
results were obtained on EdgeSim, a purpose-built simulator described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, across twelve edge nodes and five random seeds per
configuration.

\section{Limitations}
As discussed in Section~\ref{ch:discussion}, the central limitation of this work is its
reliance on synthetic rather than production traffic, and the absence of a direct empirical
comparison against a learned caching baseline. The single fixed learning rate
$\alpha = 0.15$, while selected via grid search, was not re-tuned per workload variant, and
it is plausible that drift-heavy deployments would benefit from an adaptive $\alpha$ itself.

\section{Future Work}
Three directions follow naturally from this thesis. First, validating GWC against a
production or anonymised CDN trace would address the synthetic-workload limitation directly.
Second, implementing a reference reinforcement-learning cache under the same EdgeSim harness
would allow a fair, apples-to-apples comparison of the adaptivity-versus-overhead trade-off
sketched qualitatively in Section~\ref{ch:discussion}. Third, an online mechanism for
adjusting $\alpha$ in response to detected drift -- rather than fixing it in advance -- could
recover some of the hit-ratio loss observed in Section~\ref{sec:drift-results} without
sacrificing GWC's constant-time update guarantee.

\begin{keytakeaway}[Closing Statement]
The core finding of this thesis is not that GWC is the best possible cache policy, but that a
large share of the benefit typically attributed to learned caching is available from a
constant-time, hand-designed score -- provided that score accounts for object size as well
as recency and frequency.
\end{keytakeaway}
