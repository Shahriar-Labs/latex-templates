\chapter{The Problem of Distributed Attention}
\label{ch:introduction}

Every organisation that moved its research and development work out of a
shared building and into a shared calendar made the same tacit bet: that
coordination, once handled by walking to someone's desk, could be
re-implemented in software without much loss. The bet has mostly paid off in
throughput. It has not paid off in attention. This book is about the gap
between those two ledgers.

\section{Two Kinds of Load}

Cognitive load theory\index{cognitive load theory}, in its classical
formulation, distinguishes intrinsic load -- the load imposed by a task's own
complexity -- from extraneous load, the load imposed by how the task is
presented \citep{sweller1988cognitive}. That distinction has organised four
decades of instructional-design research, and it organises most managerial
thinking about ``focus'' in the workplace too: reduce interruptions, batch
notifications, protect calendar blocks. The implicit model is that attention
is consumed by tasks and merely \emph{interrupted} by coordination.

Fieldwork at Nexa\index{Nexa}, Synapse\index{Synapse}, and
Vertex\index{Vertex} suggests the implicit model has the causal arrow
partly backwards. Coordination in a distributed team is not an
interruption of task work; for a research engineer or data scientist
juggling four asynchronous threads across three time zones, tracking
\emph{who currently owns what, and whether they know it,} is itself a
standing cognitive task -- one that runs continuously, in the background,
whether or not any single message demands a reply.\footnote{Compare the
notion of transactive memory in small groups, where knowledge of
\emph{who knows what} is itself a resource the group maintains
\citep{wegner1986transactive}. The claim of this book is that in distributed
settings the maintenance of that resource carries a measurable load of its
own.} I term this standing task \textbf{ambient coordination
load}\index{ambient coordination load}: the cost of keeping a distributed
map of ownership, status, and obligation current enough to act on.

\begin{fieldexcerpt}[title={Field note, Vertex, week 6}]
``I have Slack, I have the ticket tracker, I have two calendars because the
platform team never migrated off the old one, and I have my actual editor.
None of them agree with each other about what I'm supposed to be doing
right now. Half my day is just \emph{reconciling} them.''\\[2pt]
\hfill--- Staff engineer, platform infrastructure group
\end{fieldexcerpt}

The reconciliation this engineer describes -- checking one system of record
against another, resolving which of several partial pictures is current --
is not idle friction. Coded across the transcripts described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, it accounted for a median of 47 minutes of a
9-hour working day, concentrated in short, high-frequency bursts rather than
long blocks. Standard cognitive-load instruments, built around discrete
tasks with a clear start and end, are not well suited to measuring load that
is distributed across the day in this way.\footnote{The methodological
challenge is not new to this study; see the discussion of ``distributed
cognition in the wild'' in \citet{hutchins1995cognition}, and the extension
to computer-mediated work in \citet{hollan2000distributed}.}

\section{A Conceptual Map}

Figure~\ref{fig:model} sketches the relationship this book develops between
the two load types and the coordination scaffolding that sits between them.
Task load and ambient coordination load are not competitors for the same
fixed attentional budget in any simple additive sense; the fieldwork
suggests instead that elevated coordination load actively \emph{degrades}
the budget available for task load, through the recurring cost of context
re-establishment after every channel switch.

\section{Contributions and Chapter Map}

The book makes three contributions. First, it names and operationalises
ambient coordination load\index{ambient coordination load} as distinct from
task-level cognitive load, drawing the boundary with reference to
transactive memory theory \citep{wegner1986transactive} and situated,
distributed accounts of cognition \citep{hutchins1995cognition,
hollan2000distributed}. Second, it reports an eighteen-month, three-site
field study -- described in Part Two -- that measures this load using
instrumentation data rather than self-report alone, addressing a
long-standing reliability concern in the self-report literature. Third, it
proposes, in Part Three, a small set of organisational design levers that
the field sites themselves converged on independently, without prompting
from the research team.

Part One (Chapters~\ref{ch:introduction}--\ref{ch:literature}) lays the
conceptual groundwork. Part Two
(Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology}--\ref{ch:findings}) presents the field study.
Part Three (Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}) draws out implications for teams,
managers, and the tool-builders whose products mediate so much of this
load in the first place.
