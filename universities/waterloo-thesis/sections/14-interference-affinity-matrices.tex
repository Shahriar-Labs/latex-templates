\section{Interference Affinity Matrices}

A core contribution of this work is the \textit{interference affinity matrix} (IAM), which quantifies the performance degradation experienced when two workloads are colocated on the same node.

\vspace{6pt}
For workload types \(\tau_i, \tau_j\), the affinity coefficient
\(a_{ij} \in [0, 1]\) represents the fraction of peak throughput retained when both run on a single node:

\[
a_{ij} = \frac{\text{Throughput}_{\tau_i}^{\text{colocated}}}
               {\text{Throughput}_{\tau_i}^{\text{dedicated}}}
\]

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Measured interference affinity coefficients (mean across 64 edge nodes). Lower values indicate stronger interference.}
\label{tab:iam}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l*{4}{>{\centering\arraybackslash}X}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Video} & \textbf{Stream} & \textbf{Inference} & \textbf{Batch} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Video Analytics}  & 0.92 & 0.71 & 0.63 & 0.88 \\{}
\textbf{Stream Processing} & 0.71 & 0.95 & 0.58 & 0.91 \\{}
\textbf{Inference Serving} & 0.63 & 0.58 & 0.89 & 0.52 \\{}
\textbf{Batch Processing}  & 0.88 & 0.91 & 0.52 & 0.96 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The IAM values in Table~\ref{tab:iam} reveal a key insight: \textbf{inference workloads} suffer the most from colocation, particularly when paired with batch processing (affinity 0.52). This finding motivates dedicated inference partitions, a design choice we implement in Chapter~\ref{ch:edgesched}.
