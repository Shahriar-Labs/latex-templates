\section{Problem Statement}

Formally, given a stream of tasks $\mathcal{T} = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n\}$ arriving
at a coordinator and a set of edge nodes $\mathcal{N} = \{v_1, \dots, v_m\}$ with
time-varying capacity $c_j(\tau)$, the scheduler must produce an assignment
$\phi: \mathcal{T} \rightarrow \mathcal{N}$ that minimizes the 95th-percentile
completion latency subject to per-node capacity constraints:

\begin{equation}
\min_{\phi} \; P_{95}\big(\text{lat}(t_i)\big) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad
\sum_{t_i : \phi(t_i) = v_j} w(t_i) \leq c_j(\tau), \; \forall v_j \in \mathcal{N}
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

where $w(t_i)$ denotes the resource weight of task $t_i$. Existing schedulers
typically optimize a proxy of Equation~\ref{eq:objective} (mean latency or raw
throughput), which under bursty arrival patterns leaves the tail badly exposed.

\begin{keyboxblue}
\textbf{\color{yonseiblue}Research Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Can short-horizon load forecasting meaningfully reduce tail latency
        without adding prohibitive scheduling overhead?
  \item How should priority feedback be structured to avoid reschedule storms
        under node-failure conditions?
  \item What overhead-to-benefit ratio is acceptable for production deployment?
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyboxblue}
