Seasonal climate forecasts guide decisions in agriculture, water management and emergency
planning, and their usefulness depends on how honestly they express uncertainty. A forecast that
says a coming season will be ``slightly drier than average'' is of little help to a farmer who
needs to know the chance of a season dry enough to lose a crop. This thesis develops Bayesian
methods for forecasting monthly rainfall and temperature from historical station records and
large-scale climate indices, with the aim of producing forecasts whose probabilities can be
taken at face value.

The first contribution is a hierarchical state-space model that shares information between
nearby weather stations. Stations with short or broken records borrow strength from their
neighbours through a prior whose correlation decays with distance, which matters in Australia,
where many stations in the interior have decades of missing data. The second contribution is a
Gaussian-process component that captures slowly varying trends, including the warming trend in
temperature, without forcing them into a fixed parametric form. The third is an approximate
inference scheme, based on a variational approximation with a Kalman smoother inside it, that
scales to thousands of stations on ordinary hardware.

Forecasts are assessed with proper scoring rules and with the coverage of their prediction
intervals, rather than with point accuracy alone, because a model can be accurate on average
and still badly overconfident. Across 1,200 Australian stations and a ten-year verification
period, the hierarchical model improves the continuous ranked probability score of three-month
rainfall forecasts by 14\% relative to a climatology baseline, and its 90\% prediction intervals
contain the observed value 89\% of the time. Models fitted to each station independently are
more accurate than climatology but overconfident, with coverage of only 84\%.

The thesis concludes with a discussion of how probabilistic forecasts can be communicated to
decision makers who are not statisticians, and of the limits of purely statistical models when
the climate itself is changing and the past is an imperfect guide to the future.
