\section{Model Compression}

\subsection{Network Pruning}

Network pruning removes redundant parameters from a trained neural
network without significantly degrading its accuracy. Early work
focused on magnitude-based unstructured pruning~\cite{goodfellow2016},
which eliminates individual weights with small absolute values.
While effective at reducing parameter counts, unstructured pruning
produces sparse weight matrices that are difficult to accelerate on
commodity hardware due to irregular memory access patterns.

Structured pruning addresses this limitation by removing entire
structural components---channels, filters, or layers---yielding dense
sub-networks that map efficiently to hardware. Let
$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})$ denote the task loss and let
$\|\mathbf{W}\|_0$ be the $\ell_0$ norm (number of non-zero
parameters). The pruning problem can be formulated as:

\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \; \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\|\mathbf{W}\|_0 \leq B,
\label{eq:pruning}
\end{equation}

where $B$ is a budget on the number of parameters. Because the $\ell_0$
constraint is non-convex and combinatorial, practical methods rely on
relaxations, iterative magnitude pruning with fine-tuning, or
regularisation-based approaches that encourage sparsity during training.

\subsection{Quantisation}

Quantisation reduces the numerical precision of weights and
activations, typically from 32-bit floating-point (FP32) to 8-bit
integer (INT8) or even lower. The quantisation function for a tensor
$\mathbf{x}$ is:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_q = \operatorname{round}\!\left(
\frac{\operatorname{clip}(\mathbf{x}, \alpha, \beta) - \alpha}{s}
\right),
\qquad s = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{2^b - 1},
\label{eq:quant}
\end{equation}

where $[\alpha, \beta]$ is the clipping range, $b$ is the target
bit-width, and $s$ is the scale factor. The key challenge is determining
optimal clipping ranges that minimise the information loss introduced by
discretisation.

Quantisation-aware training (QAT)~\cite{kingma2014adam} integrates
simulated quantisation into the forward pass during training while
maintaining full-precision weights for gradient updates. This allows the
network to adapt to quantisation errors and typically yields 2--5\%
higher accuracy than post-training quantisation.

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation}

Knowledge distillation~\cite{goodfellow2016} transfers the
``dark knowledge'' captured by a large, high-capacity teacher model into
a smaller, more efficient student model. The student is trained to
match the softened output distribution of the teacher:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} =
\tau^2 \, D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(
\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_t / \tau) \;\big\|\;
\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_s / \tau)
\right),
\label{eq:distill}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{z}_t$ and $\mathbf{z}_s$ are the logits of the teacher
and student, respectively, $\tau$ is a temperature parameter that
controls the softness of the distributions, and
$D_{\text{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)$ is the Kullback--Leibler divergence.
