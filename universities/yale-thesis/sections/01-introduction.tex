\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Modern genotyping arrays and sequencing studies measure $p$ genetic variants on $n$
individuals with $p$ often a hundred times larger than $n$. The scientific question is rarely
prediction alone: investigators want to know \emph{which} variants are associated with a trait,
and how confident they can be in each discovery.

Two features of genomic data make this hard. First, the number of hypotheses is enormous, so
any procedure must control an error rate across all of them. Second, nearby variants are
inherited together, a phenomenon known as linkage disequilibrium, which makes their columns
in the design matrix highly correlated. Methods that assume weak correlation then attribute a
signal to the wrong variant, or to several at once.

This dissertation makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a block-debiased estimator whose confidence intervals keep nominal coverage under strong
        within-block correlation (Chapter~\ref{ch:method});
  \item a block knockoff filter that controls the false discovery rate at the level of linkage
        blocks rather than single variants;
  \item an empirical comparison on simulated and real expression data (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).
\end{enumerate}
