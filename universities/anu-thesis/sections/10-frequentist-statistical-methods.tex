\section{Frequentist Statistical Methods}

Generalised linear models (GLMs)~\cite{elith2009} entered the ecological
mainstream during the 1990s and quickly became the workhorse of species
distribution modelling (SDM). GLMs extend ordinary least-squares regression
to non-Gaussian response variables through a link function $g(\cdot)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glm}
  g(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]) = \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{p} \beta_k\, x_{ik},
\end{equation}
where $Y_i$ is the response (e.g.\ binary presence--absence or count) at site
$i$, and $x_{ik}$ are environmental covariates. For binary data, the canonical
logit link $g(\mu) = \log(\mu/(1-\mu))$ yields logistic regression, which
remains the most widely used SDM to this day.

Generalised additive models (GAMs) relax the linearity assumption by replacing
$\beta_k x_{ik}$ with smooth functions $f_k(x_{ik})$ estimated via penalised
splines. GAMs provide greater flexibility in capturing non-linear species--
environment relationships and have been shown to outperform GLMs on a range of
predictive metrics~\cite{elith2009}. However, both GLMs and GAMs treat
observations as conditionally independent given covariates—an assumption that
is routinely violated in spatial data due to autocorrelation arising from
dispersal limitation, unmeasured environmental gradients, and contagious
population processes.
