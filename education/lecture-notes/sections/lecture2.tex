\chapter{Quantum Measurement and Observables}

In classical mechanics, measuring a system does not affect its state. In quantum mechanics, however, measurements are active processes that alter the system's state and yield probabilistic outcomes.

\section{Projective Measurements}

A quantum measurement is described by an observable, which is a Hermitian operator \(A\) acting on the Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\). Because \(A\) is Hermitian, it has a spectral decomposition:
\[
A = \sum_{i} \lambda_i P_i
\]
where \(\lambda_i\) are the real eigenvalues of \(A\) representing the possible measurement outcomes, and \(P_i\) are the orthogonal projection operators onto the corresponding eigenspaces.
\remark{Recall that a projection operator \(P_i\) satisfies \(P_i^2 = P_i\) and \(P_i^\dagger = P_i\). They are orthogonal: \(P_i P_j = \delta_{ij} P_i\).}

\begin{definitionbox}{Projective Measurement}
A projective measurement of a state \(|\psi\rangle\) with respect to the observable \(A\) returns the outcome \(\lambda_i\) with probability given by the Born Rule:
\[
p(\lambda_i) = \langle\psi| P_i |\psi\rangle
\]
Immediately after the measurement, if the outcome \(\lambda_i\) is obtained, the state of the system collapses to:
\[
|\psi'\rangle = \frac{P_i |\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p(\lambda_i)}}
\]
\end{definitionbox}

\section{Expectation Value and Variance}

The expected outcome of a measurement on a state \(|\psi\rangle\) is the expectation value, defined as:
\[
\langle A \rangle = \langle\psi| A |\psi\rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i p(\lambda_i)
\]
\remark{The variance measures the uncertainty of the measurement outcome: \(\Delta A^2 = \langle A^2 \rangle - \langle A \rangle^2\).}

To illustrate, consider the Pauli-Z operator \(Z = |0\rangle\langle0| - |1\rangle\langle1|\), which has eigenvalues \(\pm 1\) corresponding to the states \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\).
For a general qubit state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\):
\begin{align*}
p(+1) &= \langle\psi| |0\rangle\langle0| |\psi\rangle = |\alpha|^2 \\
p(-1) &= \langle\psi| |1\rangle\langle1| |\psi\rangle = |\beta|^2
\end{align*}
The expectation value is:
\[
\langle Z \rangle = (+1)|\alpha|^2 + (-1)|\beta|^2 = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2
\]

\subsection{Comparison of Classical and Quantum Measurements}

The table below contrasts the fundamental nature of measurements in the classical and quantum regimes.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\sffamily
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXX}
\toprule
\rowcolor{bglight} \textbf{Property} & \textbf{Classical Measurement} & \textbf{Quantum Measurement} \\
\midrule
Determinism & Completely deterministic in principle. & Inherently probabilistic (Born rule). \\
Disturbance & Measurement can be done without perturbing the state. & State collapses to an eigenstate of the observable. \\
Continuous Values & Measurable quantities have continuous, smooth values. & Physical quantities (like energy, spin) can be quantized. \\
Information & Infinite information can be stored and read. & Limited readable information per measurement (Holevo limit). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Comparison of classical and quantum measurement regimes.}
\label{tab:measurement_comparison}
\end{table}

In the classical regime, we assume a objective state independent of our observation, whereas in the quantum regime, the observer and the observed system are intrinsically coupled during measurement.
