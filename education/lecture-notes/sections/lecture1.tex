\chapter{Quantum States and Superposition}

We begin our exploration of quantum information theory by formalizing the mathematical representation of quantum states. Unlike classical states, which are represented by discrete variables (bits), quantum states reside in a complex vector space equipped with an inner product.

\section{Hilbert Space and State Vectors}

A physical system in quantum mechanics is associated with a \textbf{Hilbert Space} \(\mathcal{H}\), which is a complete complex vector space with an inner product. We denote state vectors using Dirac's bra-ket notation.
\remark{Dirac notation was introduced by Paul Dirac in 1939. The ket \(|\psi\rangle\) denotes a column vector, and the bra \(\langle\psi|\) denotes its conjugate transpose (row vector).}

\begin{definitionbox}{Qubit}
A qubit (quantum bit) is the fundamental unit of quantum information. Mathematically, it is a state vector \(|\psi\rangle\) in a two-dimensional Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H} \cong \mathbb{C}^2\), represented as a linear combination:
\[
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle
\]
where \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}\) are complex probability amplitudes satisfying the normalization condition:
\[
|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
\]
The states \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\) form the computational basis.
\end{definitionbox}

\section{The Superposition Principle}

The linear structure of Hilbert space implies that quantum systems can exist in superpositions of basis states. This leads to the fundamental principle:

\begin{theorembox}{Quantum Superposition}
If \(|\psi_1\rangle\) and \(|\psi_2\rangle\) are two physically allowed states of a quantum system, then any normalized linear combination:
\[
|\psi\rangle = c_1|\psi_1\rangle + c_2|\psi_2\rangle
\]
with \(c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{C}\), is also a physically allowed state of the system.
\end{theorembox}

\subsection{Bloch Sphere Representation}

Ignoring the global phase, which has no observable consequences, any pure qubit state can be represented as a point on the surface of a three-dimensional unit sphere, known as the \textbf{Bloch Sphere}.

\remark[-1.5cm]{The parameters \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) represent the spherical coordinates of the state vector: \(\theta \in [0, \pi]\) is the polar angle, and \(\phi \in [0, 2\pi]\) is the azimuthal angle.}

We express the state vector in terms of two angles, \(\theta\) and \(\phi\):
\[
|\psi\rangle = \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|1\rangle
\]

We can visualize a slice of the Bloch sphere in the \(x\)-\(z\) plane (where \(\phi = 0\)):

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.8, >=stealth]
    \coordinate (O) at (0,0);
    \draw[thick, ->] (-1.3,0) -- (1.3,0) node[right] {\sffamily\(x\)};
    \draw[thick, ->] (0,-1.3) -- (0,1.3) node[above] {\sffamily\(z\)};
    
    % Circle
    \draw[dashed, color=slate600] (O) circle (1.0cm);
    \draw[thick, color=primary] (O) circle (1.0cm);
    
    % State vector
    \draw[very thick, ->, color=accent] (O) -- (0.6, 0.8) node[above right] {\(|\psi\rangle\)};
    
    % Angles
    \draw[fill=secondary!20] (0,0.3) arc (90:53:0.3);
    \node at (0.15, 0.45) {\(\theta\)};
    
    % Labels
    \node[above] at (0,1) {\(|0\rangle\)};
    \node[below] at (0,-1) {\(|1\rangle\)};
    \node[right] at (1,0) {\(|+\rangle\)};
    \node[left] at (-1,0) {\(|-\rangle\)};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
