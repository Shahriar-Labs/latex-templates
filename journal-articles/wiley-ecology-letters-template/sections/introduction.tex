\section{Introduction}

Soil food webs play an essential role in regulating terrestrial biogeochemical cycles, yet their structural complexity makes predicting their response to environmental perturbations a major challenge in ecology \citep{Vance2025}. Traditional models of soil systems have often treated them as monolithic structures, aggregating diverse energy channels into single compartments \citep{Sterling2024}. However, modern empirical work reveals that soil ecosystems are fundamentally multi-channel networks, fueled by two primary carbon sources: a detrital litter-derived channel (hereafter the \textit{Synapse} pathway) and a root-derived exudate channel (hereafter the \textit{Vertex} pathway) \citep{Jenkins2026}. Understanding how these channels interact, and how energy is processed by microbial consumers (\textit{Nimbus} communities) and top predators (\textit{Apex} species), is critical for predicting ecosystem resilience under global change.

The Synapse and Vertex pathways differ substantially in their chemical composition and temporal dynamics. The Synapse pathway consists of recalcitrant, particulate organic matter that enters the soil as pulses of plant litter. This material requires specialized extracellular enzymes for decomposition, resulting in slower, more sustained carbon release. In contrast, the Vertex pathway consists of highly labile, dissolved organic carbon (DOC) exuded directly by active roots. This resource is immediately available to soil microbes but exhibits high daily and seasonal variability driven by plant photosynthesis and transpiration rates.

A key theoretical question is whether the diversity of these channels stabilizes the overall soil food web. Early theoretical work by \citet{Apex2020} suggested that parallel energy channels could buffer food webs against perturbations if their inputs are uncorrelated or out-of-phase. When resource availability in one channel declines, consumers can temporarily shift their foraging effort to the alternative channel, maintaining stable populations of both intermediate and top-level predators. However, mathematical proofs of this hypothesis remain scarce, particularly for compartmental systems with distinct chemical qualities and microbial feedback loops.

In this Letter, we address this gap by developing a multi-channel compartmental model of soil food webs. We model the dynamics of carbon flow from plant inputs through litter (Synapse) and root (Vertex) pools, to microbial decomposers (Nimbus), and finally to predatory nematodes and arthropods (Apex). Our objectives are to:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\sffamily\arabic*.]
\item Formulate a mathematical representation of dual-channel soil carbon processing with adaptive microbial foraging.
\item Quantify how temporal synchrony between litter inputs and root exudation affects top-predator stability.
\item Simulate the impacts of climate-induced rainfall extremes on channel coupling and extinction risk.
\end{enumerate}
By addressing these questions, we provide a new theoretical framework for integrating plant-microbe-soil interactions into macroscopic ecosystem models, helping to bridge the gap between community ecology and biogeochemistry.
