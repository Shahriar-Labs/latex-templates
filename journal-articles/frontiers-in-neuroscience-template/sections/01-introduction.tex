\section{Introduction}

Sleep supports the stabilization and transformation of newly encoded memories. In the active systems consolidation framework, hippocampal representations are repeatedly reactivated during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and redistributed toward neocortical networks \citep{Diekelmann2010,Rasch2013}. This communication is organized by a hierarchy of rhythms: cortical slow oscillations below 1~Hz group thalamocortical sleep spindles, which in turn coordinate hippocampal sharp-wave ripples \citep{Sirota2003,Staresina2015}. Stronger temporal nesting among these events predicts better next-day memory \citep{Muehlroth2019}.

The slow oscillation is a promising target for non-invasive intervention because it can be detected online from scalp electroencephalography (EEG). Auditory clicks delivered near its depolarizing up-state can enhance slow-wave activity and, in some studies, improve declarative memory \citep{Ngo2013,Papalambros2017}. Yet outcomes vary considerably. Fixed stimulation schedules may drift from the endogenous oscillation, and control conditions often confound sound exposure with phase precision. It therefore remains unclear whether memory gains arise from phase-specific modulation or from non-specific sensory effects.

We tested whether closed-loop pink-noise pulses delivered at the predicted positive half-wave of the frontal slow oscillation would enhance overnight paired-associate retention. A phase-jittered condition used the same number and intensity of sounds but decorrelated them from the target phase. We hypothesized that phase-locked, but not phase-jittered, stimulation would (i) increase slow-oscillation amplitude, (ii) enhance fast-spindle activity and slow-oscillation--spindle coupling, and (iii) improve next-morning recall. We further predicted that individual coupling changes would explain variance in the behavioral response.
