\section{Theoretical framework}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Coherence length and upper critical field}
The superconducting coherence length sets the characteristic core size of a vortex and the length scale over which a pinning centre can interact with it. Within Ginzburg--Landau theory \cite{ginzburglandau1950} the coherence length varies with reduced temperature as
\begin{equation}
\xi(T) = \xi(0)\left(1-\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{-1/2},
\label{eq:xi}
\end{equation}
where $\xi(0)$ is the zero-temperature coherence length and $T_c$ the transition temperature of the film under consideration. The associated upper critical field follows from flux quantisation of the normal core of a single vortex,
\begin{equation}
H_{c2}(T) = \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi\xi(T)^2},
\label{eq:hc2}
\end{equation}
with $\Phi_0 = h/2e \approx 2.07\times10^{-15}$~Wb the superconducting flux quantum. Because irradiation reduces both $T_c$ and the electronic mean free path, it acts on equation~(\ref{eq:xi}) through two channels simultaneously: a lower $T_c$ raises $\xi(T)$ at any fixed measurement temperature $T<T_c$, while the concomitant reduction in mean free path drives the film toward the dirty limit, where $\xi(0)$ itself is suppressed. The competition between these channels is what ultimately determines whether irradiation raises or lowers $H_{c2}$, and by extension the vortex density the film must accommodate at a given applied field.

\subsection{Critical current density from the Bean model}
Magnetisation-based estimates of the critical current density in a slab-shaped sample follow the critical-state model of Bean \cite{bean1962}. For a thin rectangular film of in-plane dimensions $a\le b$ in a perpendicular field, the width of the magnetisation hysteresis loop $\Delta M$ (the difference between the increasing- and decreasing-field branches at fixed $H$) is related to the shielding current density by
\begin{equation}
J_c = \frac{20\,\Delta M}{a\left(1-\dfrac{a}{3b}\right)},
\label{eq:bean}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta M$ in emu~cm$^{-3}$, $a$ and $b$ in cm, and $J_c$ in A~cm$^{-2}$. We use equation~(\ref{eq:bean}) only as a cross-check against the transport-derived $I_c$ values reported in section~\ref{sec:results}; because our films are patterned into narrow Hall bars for the primary transport measurement, the transport route (current-biased, voltage-criterion $I_c$ divided by the channel cross-section) is taken as the reference value throughout, and the two methods are found to agree to within measurement uncertainty for the pristine and lightly irradiated films.

\subsection{Pinning force scaling}
The volume pinning force density $F_p = J_c B$ characterises how strongly, in aggregate, the pinning landscape resists vortex motion at a given induction $B=\mu_0 H$. A widely used empirical scaling law, due to Dew-Hughes \cite{dewhughes1974}, expresses the normalised pinning force as a function of the reduced field $h = H/H_{irr}$, where $H_{irr}$ is the irreversibility field at which measurable pinning vanishes:
\begin{equation}
\frac{F_p}{F_{p,\max}} = A\,h^{\,p}\,(1-h)^{\,q},
\label{eq:dewhughes}
\end{equation}
with $A$ a normalisation constant and the exponent pair $(p,q)$ diagnostic of the dominant pinning mechanism. Weak, spatially uncorrelated point pinning (e.g.\ isolated nitrogen-vacancy defects) is associated with $(p,q)\approx(1,2)$, while strong, extended correlated pinning by surface-normal columnar damage -- the expected signature of heavy-ion tracks -- shifts the exponents toward $(p,q)\approx(0.5,2)$, reflecting a pinning force that saturates more slowly with field because vortices can be captured along the full length of an extended track rather than at an isolated point. Fitting equation~(\ref{eq:dewhughes}) to the field-dependent pinning force extracted from each irradiated film therefore provides a mechanism-sensitive diagnostic that is independent of, and complementary to, the direct magneto-optical imaging described in section~\ref{sec:methods}.
