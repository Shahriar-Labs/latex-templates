\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central result of this study is that the useful irradiation window for enhancing $J_c$ in a $25$~nm NbN film is bounded on both sides, and that the boundary can be located quantitatively through the pinning-force exponent $p$ rather than by $J_c$ alone. Below the optimal dose, the film is under-pinned: the density of correlated defects is too low for tracks to dominate the pinning landscape, and $p$ remains close to the point-pinning value of unity. Above the optimal dose, the film is over-irradiated in the sense that matters for a fixed operating temperature of $4.2$~K -- the additional pinning sites introduced between NbN-2 and NbN-3 are, if anything, still increasing in number, but the associated drop in $T_c$ from $13.4$~K to $10.7$~K pushes the reduced temperature $T/T_c$ at the measurement point substantially higher, inflating $\xi(T)$ through equation~(\ref{eq:xi}) and softening the depairing current that ultimately bounds $J_c$ from above \cite{tinkham2004}. This is consistent with the observation that $q\approx2$ is essentially unchanged across the series: the high-field cutoff of the pinning force is set by the irreversibility field and the underlying condensation energy, not by the areal defect density, and both continue to fall with fluence even as track-pinning strength rises.

The magneto-optical imaging supports this interpretation from an independent, real-space perspective. The pristine film NbN-0 shows pronounced dendritic flux avalanches during field ramp, a hallmark of thermomagnetic instability in weakly pinned, high-thermal-conductivity superconducting films \cite{vestgarden2011}. Dendritic activity is visibly suppressed in NbN-1 and further suppressed in NbN-2, where flux entry proceeds via smooth, disordered penetration consistent with a dense array of strong pinning sites arresting avalanche growth before it can propagate. In NbN-3, dendritic-like features re-emerge at a reduced but non-negligible level, which we attribute to the lower critical current density reducing the self-field stabilisation that otherwise damps avalanche nucleation -- a secondary consequence of the same $T_c$ suppression that limits transport $J_c$ at this dose.

These findings carry direct implications for two application spaces. For SNSPDs, where dark counts are increasingly understood to originate from vortex entry at the nanowire edge under bias current close to the depairing limit, an irradiation dose in the NbN-1--NbN-2 range would be expected to raise the effective bias current at which a given dark-count rate is reached, at the cost of a few kelvin of $T_c$ margin that must be weighed against the operating temperature of the cryostat in the target system. For SRF cavities operated in the vortex-limited high-field regime, where flux trapped during cooldown is a leading source of residual surface resistance, the smoother, avalanche-free flux entry observed at the NbN-2 dose is arguably as significant as the raw $J_c$ enhancement, since avalanche-driven flux redistribution is itself a source of localised heating and hysteretic loss distinct from ordinary flux-flow dissipation.

We note two limitations of the present study. First, the pinning-force analysis assumes a single dominant mechanism at each dose, whereas in practice the pristine intrinsic disorder and the irradiation-induced tracks coexist and could in principle be more accurately separated by a two-component fit; the single-mechanism fit used here should be read as identifying the dominant contribution rather than excluding a subdominant one. Second, our irradiation series used a single ion species and energy; because the areal defect density and the individual track cross-section are coupled through the electronic stopping power at a given energy, the location of the optimal dose reported here is specific to $220$~MeV Au$^{7+}$ irradiation and would be expected to shift for a different ion or energy combination that produces a different ratio of track density to track strength.
